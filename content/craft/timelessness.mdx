You can carbon date an interface. Stitched leather means 2011. Long shadows
mean 2014. Frosted glass means 2021. Every look that arrives as a wave leaves
as one, and it takes some of your product's credibility with it.

Here is one button wearing four eras. The shape, size, and label never change.

<SurfaceErasDemo />

Only the surface moves. That is the whole lesson: **structure ages slowly,
surface ages fast.**

## What Rams left off the list

Shu Ding keeps coming back to Dieter Rams, whose principles say good design is
honest, unobtrusive, and long-lasting. Notice what is not on the list. Novel.
Current. Impressive.

Rams designed radios in the sixties that still look like they could ship
tomorrow, and he did it by refusing to make them look like the sixties.

## What actually ages well

**Structure over surface.** Hierarchy, spacing, alignment, rhythm. These read
the same in any decade. The Swiss grid is pushing eighty and nobody has
replaced it.

**Decisions shaped by function.** The Dynamic Island earned its pill. It wraps
a real sensor cutout. The imitations that copied the pill onto screens without
one kept the fashion and lost the reason, and they looked dated within months.

**Honest materials.** Text that looks like text. Buttons that look pressable.
Effects that pretend to a depth or texture the platform does not have are the
first thing to curdle.

**Restraint.** It is very hard for a monochrome, type-led interface to expire.
That is a big part of why terminal aesthetics keep coming back.

## Not a ban on style

Timelessness is not beige. It is one question, asked before adopting anything:
would this still make sense if the trend around it vanished tomorrow?

Borrow from waves freely. Just keep only the parts you can justify without
pointing at what everyone else shipped this quarter. A trend is a good reason
to examine a technique. It is never a reason to use one.
