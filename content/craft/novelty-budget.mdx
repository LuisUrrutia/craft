Say a word twenty times in a row and it turns into noise. Interfaces do the
same thing. The confetti that made you smile the first time is invisible by the
tenth and annoying by the hundredth.

Rauno Freiberg's answer is to treat novelty like money. You have a small
**novelty budget**, somewhere around ten percent of the experience, and the
question is not whether to spend it but where.

Try the little task app below in both modes. Finish a few tasks and clear a
list.

<NoveltyBudgetDemo />

When everything animates, nothing stands out and the whole thing feels a bit
slow. When only one moment animates, that moment lands.

## Spend by frequency

The rule is simple: the rarer the moment, the more expressive it can be.

- Hovering, switching tabs, checking a box. These happen constantly. Spend nothing here, or close to it.
- Opening a dialog, sending a message. Occasional. A short, quiet transition is plenty.
- Finishing onboarding, clearing your inbox, a yearly recap. Rare. This is where the budget goes.

Some moments should only ever happen once. A first-visit intro that plays
again on every reload is a bill the user keeps paying.

## Novelty also taxes learning

Every unfamiliar pattern asks people to learn your interface instead of using
what they already know. A custom scroll, a gesture nobody expects, a menu that
opens sideways. These spend the budget too, and they keep spending it every
time someone new shows up.

Spend novel interactions even more carefully than novel visuals.

## Contrast is the point

None of this means "no delight". It means a calm baseline, so the one
expressive moment has something to stand against. The quiet parts of an
interface are what make the loud part work.
