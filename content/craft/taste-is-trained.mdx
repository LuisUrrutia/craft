Taste gets talked about like a gift. Some people have the eye, the rest of us
install a component library. It is a comfortable story because it excuses not
practicing.

But nobody's sense of whether an easing is right came pre-installed. The people
we call tasteful have simply noticed more things, more carefully, for longer.
**Taste is trained.** It is a skill with reps.

Here are two cards. One of them has three things wrong with it. Take your time,
then reveal.

<SpotTheDifferenceDemo />

If you found all three, good. If you found none, also good. The point is that
you can only fix what you can see, and seeing gets better with practice.

## The training loop

**Use good software slowly.** Open the same menu three times. Ask what the
shadow is doing, why the exit is faster than the entrance, what the hover
state is not doing. Linear, Raycast, and the Family wallet are all worth an
afternoon of this.

**Say what you notice, out loud or in writing.** "This feels nice" trains
nothing. "The popover scales from the trigger, not the center" is a rep.
Vague admiration does not stick. Named differences do.

**Rebuild it, then compare.** Put your copy next to the original and study the
gap. The gap is the curriculum. Paco Coursey and Rauno Freiberg both learned
this way, and both say so.

## Change one thing at a time

Judging two options side by side is far easier than judging one on its own.
Keep the component fixed and change a single variable. Press play a few times
for each pair.

<PairJudgementDemo />

Once you can tell 150ms from 400ms without thinking, you have a reference point
you did not have before. Do this for easing, for origin, for shadow, for
spacing. Each pair adds a notch to the scale.

## Why it matters more now

When a tool can generate a plausible interface in seconds, execution stops
being the bottleneck. Picking the right one out of five plausible options is
the job, and that is taste.

The tools got faster. The eye still has to be earned.
